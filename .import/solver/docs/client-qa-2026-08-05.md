## What we are building

Two pieces:

1. **A packing solver.** Given a list of items and a list of available box types, work out which
   boxes to use and how to arrange the items inside them.
2. **A 3D visualiser.** Shows the resulting packed box so a warehouse worker can copy it.

There is also a "portal" that the client already has. The visualiser gets its own dedicated page,
not an embedded component. The client's words: "for your purposes, separate page".

We build the solver only. See [ADR-0003](decisions/0003-solver-only-scope.md).

## Hard requirements

**Inputs.** The system receives:

- A list of boxes. Each box has width, height, depth, a **maximum weight**, and a **tare weight**
  (the weight of the empty box itself).
- A list of items. Each item has a reference or ID, dimensions, and weight.
- Some additional reference data. Not specified further.

**Packing groups are a core requirement.** Certain items cannot share a box. The legal driver is
dangerous goods: different dangerous goods classes cannot be packed in the same box. The client
wants this **generic**, not hardcoded to dangerous goods, so a client could also say "put the
headphones in one box and the keyboards in another" with no safety reason at all. This will produce
a less efficient packing and that is fine, because it is a legal constraint.

**Response time: 1 to 2 seconds.** The client cited that as the accepted figure. Longer and workers
stop using it.

**Reject unpackable orders.** The client said, plainly, "I'd say reject the order. Yep, just reject
it." **[unclear]** The question this answered is not in the transcript, but the surrounding context
is items that will not fit in any available box. Confirm the exact trigger.

## Scale, and what it means for us

| Figure | Value |
|---|---|
| Concurrent users | Very few |
| Orders per day, small client | 50 |
| Orders per day, large client | 50,000 |
| Largest realistic single batch | ~1,000 |

The client explicitly said **do not build rate limiting or request throttling**. He was pushed on
"can it handle 50,000 requests" and said no, that is not the shape of the load. 50,000 is a daily
total across a whole warehouse, not a burst. 1,000 orders at once would already be "a legendary
performance from the workers".

Practical read: this is a **latency** problem, not a **throughput** problem. Optimise the solver for
a single order under 2 seconds. Do not spend the semester on horizontal scaling.

## Who actually uses it

The workflow: a picker walks the warehouse, pulls items off shelves, and brings them back. A packer
sits at a station with a label printer, a desktop, a stack of boxes, and the pile of items, and
packs them.

**Assume desktop.** They use handheld scanners for scanning, but the visualiser is looked at sitting
at the desk. Mobile would open a new avenue but is not the target. **[unclear]** He mentioned small
scanner screens as a thing they deal with, so a responsive layout is a cheap hedge, not a
requirement.

**The UX constraint that matters.** Two extremes of warehouse worker:

- Full-timers and supervisors. They know the job, they do it every day.
- Casuals. Brought in for one weekend, have never done warehouse work before and never will again.

Design for the casual. The client's test: given the context that they are seeing items placed into a
box, they should look at the screen and go "ah, I get that". No training.

## What the client wants to see

On the solver, in his priority order:

1. **Efficiency.** The main thing.
2. **Extensibility.** He said this repeatedly and unprompted. The end goal is that this gets
   developed further after we are done. He specifically named object-oriented design and gave an
   example: a box abstraction that handles different **kinds** of box, so a shipping container and a
   small parcel are the same type of thing. That, he noted, also solves the top-heavy and stacking
   questions later without a rewrite.

On the visualiser:

3. **User experience.** The big one. Present the information so it is easy to understand and easy to
   act on.

He also lowered the bar deliberately: this is a research prototype, not the shipping product. "You
don't have to make a very exciting place." Do not gold-plate the UI.

**Visualiser features he asked for, both wanted:**

- View the packed box **in totality**, 3D, orbit around it and look from different angles.
- View the packing **step by step**, item one, then item two on top of it. He called this "really
  beautiful" and a student compared it to an instruction manual. This is the differentiator.

## Modes: the extensibility story

Output should be identical regardless of who the user is. But the client would love to see
**multiple modes**, and this is the clearest extensibility hook in the whole session:

- **Standard mode.** The core problem. Given a box, find the optimal arrangement of items in it.
  Real geometry, real fit.
- **Fluid mode.** For actual fluids, and also for apparel and anything squishable that fills air
  gaps. Ignore geometry entirely: the box has X cubic space, the items total Y cubic space, if
  Y fits in X the worker shoves it in. **[unclear]** He named this "fluid mode" as something they
  already have in house.
- **Own-packaging / no box.** Some items ship in their own packaging. A bulk pack of something goes
  out with a label slapped on it, no box at all. **[unclear]** The example is garbled but the rule
  is clear: some orders bypass packing.

"Different modes and different algorithms" is his stated future direction. Even if we only build
standard mode, the architecture should make the other two obvious drop-ins. That is exactly what he
means by extensibility, and it is free marks if the design shows it.

## Explicitly out of scope

Do not build these. He said so.

- **Access control and auth.** Robust access control already exists on their side. Access is at the
  level of the model as a whole. Not our problem.
- **Fragile and this-way-up handling.** Not a real consideration. Fragile items do not get packed
  alongside other things in reality, they get handled separately. He called it out of scope but
  "would be really cool to see". Stretch goal at best.
- **Box weight limits and tare weight in the total.** There is a real rule that boxes cannot exceed
  a certain weight, and a 1 kg empty box eats into that limit. He said they are not running into
  this yet, but will. Note it in the design, do not implement it.
- **Rate limiting and request throttling.** Covered above.

## Assumptions the client handed us

- **Dimensions are captured accurately.** "At some point you've got to trust the data." We do not
  need to handle bad measurements.
- **Box dimensions should be internal, not external.** He flagged that box dimensions ought to
  account for the wall thickness, so the number we pack against is the usable interior, not the
  outside of the box. He called this a consideration to work out. Worth pinning down.

## Things he offered us

- A **site visit** to see the factory or the system in operation. He said he can organise it.
- **YouTube videos** they already made as marketing material, showing the process. He said he would
  send them through.
- Possibly **simulated data** to work against. **[unclear]** The question asked for it, his answer
  is garbled, but he did not say no.

Someone should chase all three. The videos are the cheapest and probably the most useful.

## Open questions for the next client session

He is back two weeks after this session. Bring these:

1. What exactly triggers "reject the order"? An item too big for every box, or something else?
2. Box dimensions: are the numbers we will receive internal or external? If external, do we get wall
   thickness?
3. Can we get the simulated dataset, the videos, and a site visit? Who do we chase?
4. What is the format of the item and box data? A file, an API, a paste-in? What does the portal
   hand us?
5. How many box types does a typical warehouse stock? This drives whether box selection is a search
   or a trivial loop.
6. Does rotation of items count? Can the packer turn an item on its side, or do items have a fixed
   orientation?
7. Are the packing group rules pairwise ("A cannot go with B") or class-based ("class 3 cannot go
   with class 5")? This changes the data model.
8. For fluid mode, is it purely volumetric, or is there a fill factor?

Questions 6 and 7 are the two that will actually block implementation. Ask those first.
[`client-requirements.md`](client-requirements.md) lists the same questions in that priority order,
with what our contract currently assumes for each.
